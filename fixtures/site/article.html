The seed library that outgrew its shelf

By the Riverbend Gazette staff. Corrections go to desk@news.example.com.

Three springs ago the Riverbend branch library cleared one shelf near the front door and filled it with paper envelopes of seeds. Anyone with a card could take up to five packets, plant them, and bring seeds back at the end of the season if the plants did well.

Nobody on staff expected the shelf to matter much. It was meant to fill a gap left by a rack of old magazines.

By the end of the first season the shelf had lent out a little over four hundred packets. Beans and squash went first, then tomatoes, then a long tail of herbs that people asked for by name.

The returns were thinner, about one packet back for every six that went out. The staff kept every returned envelope in a labeled box and wrote down who grew what and where.

The second year the library moved the seeds to a full bookcase and added a sign-up sheet for a monthly swap. The swap brought in growers who had never borrowed a book from the branch.

Several of them came for the seeds and left with library cards. That's the number the branch manager now leads with when the town council asks what the program costs.

The records also showed something nobody planned for. Seeds that came back from local gardens did better the next year than the packets bought from a catalog.

Volunteers started marking the returned envelopes with a small green dot so borrowers could pick the local ones on purpose. By the third season most of the bean and squash stock on the shelf carried the dot.

The library now runs the shelf with two volunteers and a budget smaller than its old magazine order. The next step is a printed guide to saving seeds from each crop, written by the growers who return the most packets.

The branch plans to keep the guide free and to put a copy in every envelope that goes out next spring.
